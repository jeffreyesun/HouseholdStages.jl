# Ben-Porath human capital — life cycle (Huggett–Ventura–Yaron 2011)

A Part-3 demonstration that a literature human-capital household block is **one
existing library stage**, with **no bespoke household stage** rolled in the
example. From human capital `h` the agent picks next-period `h'`, paying a convex
effort cost on gross investment and earning the rental flow `R·h`; the
cross-section over `h` is generated by **age** — the Huggett–Ventura–Yaron (2011)
life-cycle form of the Ben-Porath (1967) technology.

## Household block

| Stage (time order) | Library stage | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Invest in human capital | `CapitalInvestmentStage(:h)` | From `h`, choose next `h'`; pay `effort_cost(i)` on gross investment `i = h' − (1−δ)h`, earn `production(h) = R·h`. |

That is the entire within-period household problem — a single
`ContinuousArgmaxStage` (`CapitalInvestmentStage` is its human-capital wrapper) on the
`:h` axis. The block is assembled in `model.jl` via `human_capital_household`,
with `mean_h` and `mean_earnings` moments attached.

### Why one stage, no shock, no second axis

- **No shock / no second axis.** Ben-Porath's choice is deterministic and the
  policy `h ↦ h'(h)` is monotone, so an infinite-horizon stationary sweep would
  collapse the cross-section to a point mass. The **life-cycle (finite-horizon)**
  structure is what spreads `h` across the population — and it is exactly HVY.
- **Ability is a profile in `env`, not a state axis.** `CapitalInvestmentStage`'s
  `production`/`effort_cost` are `(value; env)` closures that build an `(h', h)`
  reward with no second-axis dependence, so a Markov ability *state* would be
  economically inert through the stage. The age-specific **ability profile**
  `a_t` (the HVY declining ability-to-produce-human-capital) instead enters
  through `env` each age — that is driver logic, not a household stage.

### The cost function

New human capital `Q = a·(s·h)^γ` from time share `s` at ability `a`. To deliver
gross investment `i` the agent forgoes earnings on `s·h = (i/a)^{1/γ}` time:

    effort_cost(i; env) = env.R · (i / env.a)^{1/γ},   production(h; env) = env.R · h.

With `γ ∈ (0,1)` the cost is convex (exponent `1/γ > 1`), so the reward
`R·h − effort_cost(h' − (1−δ)h)` is supermodular in `(h', h)` and the
`:divide_conquer` monotone solve is valid. Higher ability `a` lowers the cost —
the engine of the life cycle.

## Equilibrium notes

Partial equilibrium: the rental rate `R` is exogenous, so there is no market to
clear. The "outer loop" is the life-cycle solve itself, rolled in
`steady_state.jl` as **driver** logic:

1. **Backward induction** (ages `N_age … 1`): from a zero terminal continuation,
   seat each age's policy at its env `(; R, a = ability_at_age(t))` via the
   library `backward!`.
2. **Forward cohort sweep** (ages `1 … N_age`): a unit point mass born at `h0` is
   pushed through the age-indexed policies via the library `forward!`. The age-`t`
   marginal is unit mass; the marginal over all ages is the **hump-shaped
   life-cycle human-capital profile** — the non-degenerate cross-section.

## How to run

```julia
julia --project=. examples/human_capital/steady_state.jl
```

Prints cohort-mass conservation, value finiteness, and the age profile of mean
human capital. At the default calibration (`N_age = 40`, `γ = 0.7`, `δ = 0.05`):
human capital starts near `h0 ≈ 2.0`, rises to a peak of ≈ 12.7 around age 9
(heavy early investment while ability is high and the horizon long), then
declines as depreciation dominates once ability tapers — the classic Ben-Porath
hump.

Regression test:

```julia
julia --project=. -e 'using HouseholdStages; include("test/test_example_human_capital.jl")'
```
